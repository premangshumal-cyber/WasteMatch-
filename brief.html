01 / EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

What is being offered?

WasteMatch is a proposed B2B business-service approach to identifying, screening and facilitating viable industrial material reuse opportunities. It targets an overlooked commercial decision: whether one organization’s surplus can be technically acceptable and economically attractive to another.

The concept is available for exploratory discussions on outright acquisition of agreed concept materials, defined-scope licensing or paid co-development. It is not an operating platform, and it has no asserted customers, revenues, exclusive technology rights or proven savings.

Investment thesis to test

An existing industrial group, recycling operator or procurement network may be better placed than a standalone new venture to pilot a feasibility-led resource exchange service using its established counterparties and compliance capabilities.

02 / POSITIONING

Why this is not pitched as a conventional marketplace.

The industrial symbiosis field is established and numerous platforms already trade surplus resources. WasteMatch therefore does not claim to have invented waste exchange, AI material matching or circular commerce.

The proposed positioning is a service-led qualification method organized around three gates: (1) material suitability, (2) delivered unit economics and (3) commercial and regulatory execution readiness. A vetted opportunity brief, rather than a raw listing, is the intended service output.

Whether this positioning is sufficiently distinctive or valuable would need direct comparison against competitors, interviews with potential operators and pilot evidence.

03 / OPERATING DESIGN

Transferable workflow proposal.

  1. Intake: Register non-hazardous material candidates with grade, volume, origin, consistency, documentation and disposal baseline.
  2. Compatibility: Research possible industrial applications; specify product standards, substitution limits and laboratory assessment where relevant.
  3. Economics: Calculate avoided disposal, agreed transfer price, input-cost displacement, handling, transport and testing costs without double counting internal transfers.
  4. Readiness: Screen counterparties, buyer acceptance, permit requirements, liabilities, traceability and transport distance.
  5. Facilitation: Facilitate a scoped, documented pilot between authorized participants and record actual results.

These are proposed business-process steps. No proprietary matching software or legally reviewed operating manual is represented as complete.

04 / VALIDATION ROADMAP

An operator-led 90-day pilot hypothesis.

DAY 01–30

Discovery and screening

Recruit willing participants from an existing industrial cluster, obtain consent and material records, define safety boundaries, shortlist candidate flows.

DAY 31–60

Technical and financial diligence

Document grades, permissible reuse, required testing, total delivered costs, demand assumptions and practical logistics.

DAY 61–90

Controlled pilot and decision

Where approvals permit, test one exchange, measure actual costs and performance, and decide whether to continue, revise or discontinue.

The 90-day outline is an illustrative planning horizon; testing, authorizations and counterparties may require longer. No pilot has been completed.

05 / COMMERCIAL STRUCTURES

Transaction terms are negotiable.

Asset / concept purchase

Define ownership, assignable assets, deliverables, warranties, transition cooperation, payment and post-transfer use.

Limited license

Specify licensed materials, territory, term, exclusivity, permitted uses, improvements, royalties and termination rights.

Paid development

Agree staged deliverables, acceptance criteria, fees, ownership of outputs and a separately negotiated commercialization option.

There is no advertised asking price or implied valuation because the concept lacks validated financial performance and a formal IP assessment.

06 / DUE DILIGENCE

What a serious buyer should verify.

Demand: Confirm that industrial operators will pay for a qualified exchange-identification service, rather than rely on established exchanges.

Technical suitability: Prove that substitute materials meet the receiver’s real specifications with reliable supply.

Economics: Include transport distance, quality loss, testing, handling, working capital, taxes, contractual risk and service acquisition cost.

Regulation: Confirm which materials, operators, transporters and processing activities require permits and authorizations under applicable law.

Intellectual property: Check prior art, any protectable original expression, brand availability, trademark searches and transferable ownership; a general idea is not inherently exclusive.

Commercial proof: Seek interviews, signed expressions of interest and repeatable pilot economics before assigning a meaningful valuation.

This is a commercial concept brief, not legal, engineering, safety or investment advice. No regulatory approval or exclusivity is claimed.
